# The night we almost left the Outer Space Treaty

*Post 3 of the Designated Lunar Areas series. Post 1 introduced the DLA framework. Post 2, Geology Writes the Rules, showed why the south pole concentrates everyone onto the same few sites. This one is about what happened when we put people on those sites.*

Two hours and nineteen minutes into our July 1 session of Overlap, a player representing the LRC bloc threatened to withdraw from the Outer Space Treaty.

Nobody scripted that. There is no withdrawal mechanic in the game. He reached for it because the negotiation had cornered him, and treaty leverage was the only card left on the table. Across the call, the Concordium side scrambled to check what withdrawal would even mean. Our reply, in role, ended up being the most useful sentence of the night: we are not suggesting you leave the Outer Space Treaty. We are asking that you uphold it and not claim the land.

That exchange is why I build games instead of only writing analysis. You cannot survey your way to the moment a person discovers what treaty leverage feels like. You can only put them in a position where they need it.

## The session

Overlap is a governance simulation of the lunar south pole, built on real LRO terrain, where every asset projects a three-tier keep-out zone based on Christine Tiballi's DLA framework. Two blocs, an Artemis-style coalition and an ILRS-style program, compete over water ice for twelve rounds. The full session exports to a reconstruction file: every asset, every ring, every rover trace, and a 181-event log. The tabletop is a data-collection instrument that happens to be fun.

The July 1 arc, in one paragraph. LRC landed hard and built fast, and led Concordium on infrastructure for essentially the whole game, 3,300 asset points to 2,505 by the end. Then it maxed out its safety zones, refused coordination, dropped assets against our boundaries as a wedge, and declared, in character, that its habitats and thus its buffer zones are considered territories for the purposes of its security. The facilitator priced all of it. Rulings and injects put LRC's policy ledger at minus 800 by the final round. Concordium banked 230 kilograms of ice, held the diplomatic line, and won 2,875 to 2,540.

The scoreboard sentence writes itself: LRC out-built us the entire game and lost anyway. Institutions beat industry in this session.

## What the rings did

The headline that matters for DLA policy is a number that never moved: safety violations, zero, both sides, all twelve rounds.

That was not because nobody got close. The session export logs every cross-bloc separation against the coordination rings, and the entries into those rings are exactly where the interesting politics happened. One of them produced an ultimatum. Another produced a convened Security Council session where the two blocs negotiated a fifty-fifty split of Haworth crater, drawn along the line of who was already where. The rings did not prevent contact. They converted contact into procedure: an entry triggered a conversation, the conversation produced a deal, and the deal held.

That is the DLA thesis working as designed. A zone that is too small protects nothing. A zone that is too large becomes territory, which is precisely the move LRC tried, and precisely the move the scoring punished. The playable middle, where zones are big enough to trigger coordination and small enough to leave room for a neighbor, is the same 0.5 to 2 kilometer band my SciTech geometry says is the only range where multiple actors fit in the major basins at all.

## Diplomacy is measurable

One more thing the session produced, almost by accident. The call transcript has timestamps, which means the negotiation itself is data. Utterances per minute across the two hours trace the whole arc: the early land rush, the ultimatum, the convened council, the treaty threat, the deal, the debrief. Serious-games researchers talk about capturing negotiation dynamics; it turns out a meeting transcript and twenty lines of parsing get you a first-order instrument.

## What I am taking into the framework

Three things, stated plainly.

First, safety-becomes-territory is not a hypothetical failure mode. A player found it in under an hour, unprompted, and said the quiet part in full sentences. Any DLA regime has to price zone inflation, because someone will try it on day one.

Second, unsized zones are the vulnerability. Every dangerous move in the session lived in the gap the Artemis Accords leave open by declining to name a number. The precedents are sub-kilometre, the tiers Christine proposes are sub-kilometre, and the geometry caps the workable range near 2.5 kilometers. Any number beats no number.

Third, the convene mechanic earned its place. The single most requested feature from our June playtest, a moment where people talk, turned out to be where the entire session got decided. If your governance framework has no equivalent of a room, the pressure finds another outlet. Ours found the Outer Space Treaty.

At the end of the night, in the debrief, one player was workshopping a catchphrase and landed on the question this whole fellowship is trying to answer: how do you write rules for a game you have never played?

You play it first.
